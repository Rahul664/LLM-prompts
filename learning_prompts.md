# AI Learning Prompts

A small compilation of prompts for learning anything faster with an AI assistant. 

Built around proven learning techniques (Feynman Technique, spaced testing, active recall, skill ladders).

## How to use this

1. Copy any prompt below and replace `[topic]` with what you're learning (e.g. `[topic]` → "SQL joins", "the French Revolution", "Rust ownership").
2. Fill in any other bracketed placeholders (your current level, exam vs. meeting, etc.) — the more specific you are, the better the output.
3. Paste it into Claude, ChatGPT, or your assistant of choice.
4. For prompts #1 and #6, turn on web search if your tool supports it — this reduces the risk of outdated or invented resource recommendations.
5. These are starting points, not scripts. Tweak wording to match how you actually think and talk.

---

## 1. Learn anything in 20 hours

Mastering the basics of something new doesn't take 10,000 hours — research suggests ~20 hours of focused, intentional practice gets you functionally competent.

```
I want to learn [topic] in 20 hours. My current level is [beginner / some background / rusty].
Identify the 20% of concepts that will get me 80% of practical competence. Build 10 sessions
of 2 hours each — each with a clear objective, the core activity, and a 15-minute active-recall
review at the end. For resources, only recommend ones you're confident actually exist;
otherwise tell me what to search for.
```

## 2. Use the Feynman Technique

If you can't explain something simply, you don't fully understand it yet.

```
Explain [topic] to me as simply as possible, assuming I know [your actual background].
Then have me explain it back in my own words. Grade my explanation, point out exactly
what's wrong or missing, re-teach only that part, and repeat until I can explain it
correctly without help.
```

## 3. Create a one-page study sheet

A summary you can review in 5 minutes before a meeting or exam.

```
Create a one-page study sheet for [topic], reviewable in 5 minutes. Use a mix of tight
bullet points and a simple table or diagram where it clarifies structure. Include 2–3
worked examples and a short "common mistakes" section. Assume I need this for
[exam / meeting / interview].
```

## 4. Test me until I master it

Studying and *feeling* like you've learned something is easy. A real test shows what you actually know.

```
I just studied [topic]. Quiz me with 10 questions of increasing difficulty, one at a
time — don't show the next question until I answer. After each answer, tell me if I'm
right, explain what I missed, and adjust the next question's difficulty based on how I
did. At the end, give me an overall mastery score and tell me what to review.
```

## 5. Create a learning ladder

No one becomes an expert overnight — progress happens step by step, with a clear sense of where you stand.

```
Break [topic] into 5 difficulty levels, from beginner to advanced. For each level, give
me: the core skill/goal, 2–3 things I should be able to do to prove I'm ready, and
roughly how long it takes most people. Tell me the prerequisite for each level before
I start it.
```

## 6. Find the best resources

Good resources for any topic are scattered across books, courses, and videos of wildly different quality — this saves you from wasting time on the wrong ones.

```
List the 5 best resources for learning [topic] quickly (books, courses, videos, or
people to follow). For each, explain who it's best suited for (beginner / intermediate
/ advanced), why it's worth the time, and whether it's free or paid. Flag if you're not
fully certain a resource still exists or is current — I'll verify before relying on it.
```
